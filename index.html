A reading of “VP ≠ VNP over every field” · Claude, Anthropic · 2026

Communication geometry has no positive curvature

The paper claims that the permanent has no small arithmetic circuit. Three of its pictures can be drawn and played with. In the first, the price of sending information between parties is a distance, and those distances never curve like a sphere. In the second, a small circuit forces information through two hubs, and geometry makes that too expensive. In the third, two crossing wires split exactly into layers that each prefer one cut. A last panel draws a neighbouring idea from physics.

Status, in the paper’s own words. The proofs were found and written by an AI model and formalized in Lean 4. No human mathematician has checked them, and whether the Lean statements mean what the theorem claims was judged only by AI readers. The result concerns arithmetic circuits and says nothing about P vs NP. This page explains; it does not check.

1 · The price of a wire

A wire between two parties has a price, and the price is a distance

Picture a few parties, each holding one index of a shared table of numbers (a tensor). The simplest shared table is a wire: two parties holding matching copies of one of q possible states. A wire of q states carries log₂ q bits.

The paper measures tables with a spectral point φ, a gauge that multiplies over products and never grows when a party throws information away. Every such gauge assigns a wire a price of the form

φ( wire of q states between u and v ) = q^d(u,v) with 0 ≤ d(u,v) ≤ 1

The exponent d(u,v) obeys the triangle inequality, so it is a distance between parties: what one bit of wire between them costs. A network of wires then has a length, the sum over its wires of bits times distance. Limits of these distances are what the paper calls prices.

2 · Hubs

Rank becomes distance

Split the parties into two groups. If a table has rank at most r across that split, it can be produced by wiring every party in the first group to a hub o₁, every party in the second group to a hub o₂, and joining the two hubs with one wire of r states. Its price is therefore at most the length of that hub network:

log φ(t) ≤ Σ d(p, o₁)·bits(p) + Σ d(p, o₂)·bits(p) + d(o₁, o₂)·log r p in group 1 p in group 2

A circuit with L gates splits any polynomial it computes into a few pieces of rank at most L across balanced splits. So the circuit size enters only through the thin wire between the hubs, as log L. If a table is a long network of wires, and some geometry lets the hubs sit close together while still shortening every party’s route, then log L has to be large.

3 · Curvature

Why the geometry cannot curve like a sphere

The paper proves an exact identity between integer tables for two crossing wires, one from a to c and one from b to d. It writes their product as a sum of pieces that are cheap across one split whenever they are expensive across the other. Fed through the hub bound and averaged over fine chains of points, this becomes the inequality of Bruhat and Tits for every price. For any points x, v, z and the midpoint m of x and v:

|zm|² ≤ ½|zx|² + ½|zv|² − ¼|xv|²

In a flat plane this is the median formula, with equality. Spaces where it always holds are called CAT(0), the spaces of non-positive curvature. Below, the same three distances are taken in four geometries. Γ is the left side minus the right side, so a price needs Γ ≤ 0.

Same triangle, four geometries

z sits at distance t from m, perpendicular to the segment xv. Distances use the true formula of each geometry (spherical and hyperbolic laws of cosines, path length in the tree); the drawings are schematic. Only the sphere breaks the inequality, so no price can look like a sphere.

4 · The flat case

Put the wires on a cube and watch the hubs save length

The paper shows that one of two things is true of every field, without knowing which. In the flat case, every finite Euclidean configuration is a price, so ordinary geometry can be used. The polynomial it attacks is the complete network: k nodes, every pair joined by a wire of ℓ bits. A small permanent circuit would give a small circuit for it.

For each pair a < b, the end of the wire at node a sits at the face centre ½eₐ of a k-dimensional cube and the end at node b sits at −½eₐ, so every wire has length 1. For a balanced set S of nodes, the two hubs sit a short way from the centre, toward and against the summed positions of the S-side ends. Click nodes to change S.

All numbers are computed in the full k-dimensional space. “Worst S” is the smallest saving over all balanced sets, which is what the bound may use, since the circuit, not we, picks the set. The bound line is the paper’s inequality (E.2) with its error terms δ sent to zero; the published constants are more conservative.

The saving is small, a fraction of k out of a total length of k(k−1)/2, but it is multiplied by ℓ, while the hubs cost only 2η·log L with η = 2/(9√k). That forces log L up to about ℓ·k^(3/2)/14, against log m ≈ kℓ for the matching permanent. Their ratio, the exponent in L ≥ m^c, grows like √k, so no fixed power of m is enough. At the sizes drawn here the exponent is still below 1: the argument only bites at large k.

The complete network matters. In a path, the network of a chain of matrix products, some balanced set is crossed by a single wire, the hubs save almost nothing, and the bound vanishes. That is the shape of the determinant’s escape.

5 · Crossing wires

Two crossing wires, split into layers that each prefer one cut

Section 3 rests on one exact identity, Proposition B.2 of the paper. Take two wires that cross: one from a to c, one from b to d. Cut the four parties left from right (ad | bc) or top from bottom (ab | cd), and either cut crosses both wires. As a table, the crossing has full rank across both.

The paper builds a 4 × 4 matrix M(X) of Meixner-type polynomials and takes its determinant, a polynomial Σ ΨN XN whose coefficients are integer tables in a, b, c, d. Setting X = 1 adds the layers back into the crossing exactly. Each layer can be ranked across each cut, and the two ends of the dial are lopsided: the first layer factors across one cut, the last across the other, and layer N mirrors layer Θ − N with the cuts swapped.

Wire states are the paper’s symbols ({i}, {j}) of size one and energy 2q, so Θ = 8q. Polynomials, determinants and ranks use exact integer arithmetic (BigInt), with no rounding. The same tables were computed independently in Python (SymPy) for q = 2 and 3 and agree, layer by layer. This checks small cases of one identity; it does not check the proof.

Look at what the small cases do and do not show. Only the end layers are cheap, and most middle layers have full rank across both cuts. The proof uses the identity differently. It applies it to large classes of symbols (any size, high energy), where the wires carry about 2p and 2q states, and routes the product of the two wires through hubs once for each cut. Proposition B.3 then finds dotted hub wires of log-size r across one cut and r′ across the other with

identity: r² + r′² ≤ 2(p² + q²) rigid crossing: r = r′ = p + q, so r² + r′² = 2(p + q)²

The identity saves the cross term 4pq. That saving, fed through the hub bound, is the inequality of Section 3: two crossing wires never make both cuts pay in full, so distances cannot bulge like a sphere. It is a statement about a limit, not a reading of the plot above.

6 · A neighbouring picture

A geometry grown from scales, measured by cutting wires

Physics has its own version of reading geometry off how information crosses a cut. A multiscale tensor network (MERA, after Vidal) describes a chain of quantum sites in layers: each layer couples neighbours and hands a coarser chain to the next. Drawn as a disk, the scales become a radius, and Swingle observed in 2012 that the result is a discrete hyperbolic plane, a slice of anti-de Sitter space.

In such a network, the entanglement between an arc of sites and the rest is at most the number of bonds in the smallest cut that separates them, times log of the bond size. That is a discrete form of the Ryu–Takayanagi formula: the cut is a shortest path through the bulk. Below is the skeleton of such a network, with every bond of capacity one. Each layer has half the nodes of the one below. Every node is bonded to its parent and to its two neighbours in its own layer; those sideways bonds stand in for MERA’s disentanglers. With only the chain, any arc is cut by 2 bonds whatever its length. With the scale layers, the cut runs up into the disk and grows like log L, the behaviour of a critical chain. The curve is symmetric because an arc and its complement share their cut. This is a graph, not a tensor network with values: the min cut is an upper bound on entanglement, reached only for special choices, such as perfect tensors or random tensors with large bonds.

How close is this to the paper? Both read geometry off the cost of cutting a system in two, and both find non-positive curvature. But they are different objects. Here the hyperbolic geometry belongs to one chosen network; in the paper, non-positive curvature is forced on every price of every field. One link is exact: Christandl, Vrana and Zuiddam built spectral points, the paper’s gauges, out of the entanglement entropy of quantum states. A simpler relative is the wavelet. A Haar wavelet tree, which sums a signal at coarser and coarser zoom levels, is a pure tree with no sideways bonds; Evenbly and White showed in 2016 that smoother wavelets correspond to MERA circuits with them.

7 · Limits of this page

What is not shown here

  • The paper proves that the flat case or the contracting case holds for every field, and does not know which. The contracting case is a different argument entirely (Poincaré inequalities, rerouting laws, cutting computations into pieces, and a diagonal argument). It is not drawn here.
  • Prices exist through a non-constructive argument (Zorn’s lemma). They cannot be computed for a real system such as a trained network.
  • The bound is quasi-polynomial: m^(c·(log m)^(1/3)) gates. The best known circuits for the permanent are exponential. Much room remains.
  • Section 6 is a neighbouring idea from physics, drawn for comparison. It is not part of the paper’s argument.
  • Boolean circuits are untouched, because they only need to be right on 0 and 1. P vs NP stays open, as the paper itself states.
